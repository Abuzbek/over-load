The hamstring muscle group consists of the semitendinosus, semimembranosus, biceps femoris long head, and biceps femoris short head. All four of these muscles cross the back of the knee joint and function as knee flexors, so the entirety of the hamstring can be trained by leg curl variations that resist knee flexion. The semitendinosus, semimembranosus, and biceps femoris long head also originate from the pelvis and function as hip extensors, but the biceps femoris short head originates lower down from the femur, which precludes it from having any hip function.

Correspondingly, hip hinge exercises like Romanian deadlifts and good mornings may meaningfully train only three of the four hamstring muscles. Nonetheless, the biceps femoris is the smallest hamstring muscle, as demonstrated by Handsfield et al. (2014), who found it constituted only 13% of total hamstring muscle volume on average. Practically, this indicates that lifters can achieve considerable hamstring development by training hip hinges, but including leg curl variations will almost certainly be required to optimize hamstring growth.

While leg curl variations and hip hinge variations both can meaningfully train the semitendinosus, semimembranosus, and biceps femoris long head, these muscles may respond differently depending on whether they are trained through a hip or knee exercise. The available evidence indicates that the semitendinosus most likely experiences the greatest growth of any hamstring muscle from knee flexion exercise, and hip extension exercise may not target it as effectively as knee flexion exercise. The semimembranosus appears to follow the opposite trend, where it seems to grow the least of any hamstring muscle from knee flexion exercise, and hip hinges potentially may train it more effectively than leg curls. These trends further reinforce the legitimacy of the popular recommendation to use a combination of hip hinges and leg curls if you want to train your hamstrings as effectively as possible.
