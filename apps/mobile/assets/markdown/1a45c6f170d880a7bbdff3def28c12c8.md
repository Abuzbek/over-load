## Setting up
1. After initially standing in the center of the captain’s chair with your back facing its back pad, step up onto its foot plates while placing your hands against the arm pads for any balance assistance as needed.
2. Bend your elbows and place the bottom of your forearms onto the arm pads while firmly grasping the handles with your palms facing in toward each other.
    1. Your back should be contacting the backrest while your torso is in an upright position.
3. While bearing the force from your bodyweight through your elbows into the arm pads, pick your feet up off the foot plates and allow them to hang straight down toward the ground.
    1. Keep your shoulder blades and shoulders near the natural position in which they are when normally standing upright, rather than letting your shoulder blades and shoulders shrug up from gravity pulling your torso down.
    2. You should be able to draw a straight line vertically down from your torso through your legs.
## Exercise technique
1. March up both knees toward your chest through your available range of motion while curling your hips forward and allowing your knees to progressively bend as they lift higher up.
    1. Curling your hips forward will round your lower back and result in your buttocks facing forward at the end of the lifting phase.
    2. You do not consciously need to think about progressively bending your knees since they will naturally bend when marching your knees up unless you intentionally try to straighten them.
    3. How closely you are able to raise your knees up toward your chest will be influenced by the mobility in your hips and lower back, along with your strength.
    4. Whenever you feel like you cannot bring your knees any closer toward your chest, you have reached the end of the lifting phase.
    5. Keep your upper body stationary.
2. Reverse the movement in a controlled manner by allowing your knees to descend while uncurling your lower back until you have returned to the starting position with your thighs directed straight down toward the ground.
    1. Actively lower your legs throughout the descent rather than letting gravity rapidly pull you back down, which could result in your legs swinging back behind the starting position as momentum takes over.
    2. Your knees can naturally transition to a less bent position as you lower your legs.
3. Alternate between steps 1 and 2 until you have completed your set and are ready to return your feet onto the foot plates and step down from the captain’s chair.
## Description
This exercise can effectively develop your [rectus abdominis](../Muscles/Rectus%20Abdominis%201955c6f170d880ffa74fdc1ed9f102dd.md) while also training your [obliques](../Muscles/Abdominal%20Obliques%201b95c6f170d880f5bdc1c2ca4cd25d5f.md) and [hip flexors](../Muscles/Primary%20Hip%20Flexors%202845c6f170d8808187a5fdcaf3da8985.md).

### Straight knee leg raise versus bent knee leg raise

With the exception of utilizing a straighter knee position, the technique for the straight knee leg raise is otherwise essentially the same as the bent knee leg raise, so both variations primarily train the same muscle groups. In terms of the training effect these exercises provide, the main difference is that the straight knee leg raise is a higher intensity variation due to how it shifts the center of mass of your legs farther forward relative to your trunk and hip. This results in a longer lever arm of resistance acting on your trunk and hip, which requires your abdominal muscles and hip flexor muscles respectively generating greater trunk flexion and hip flexion torque to overcome. Resistive torque can be increased by increasing the weight you move or by increasing the lever arm in which the weight acts on a particular joint, so the straight knee leg raise can function similar to a weighted knee raise without requiring additional weights. The greater magnitude of resistance from the straight knee leg raise almost certainly explains why McGill et al. (2014) found this variation to yield noticeably greater EMG activity of the abdominal muscles than the bent knee leg raise when participants performed three reps of each exercise.

Nonetheless, this does not mean that the straight knee leg raise is inherently superior to the bent knee leg raise for developing the abdominal muscles in the same way that squatting 200lb (91kg) is not necessarily better than squatting 150lb (68kg) for inducing quad hypertrophy. If you have the strength to perform both leg raise variations with sound technique, you simply will have to perform greater reps per set with the bent knee position compared to the straight knee position to achieve the same hypertrophic stimulus. If an equal number of sets are performed until the same proximity failure (e.g., performing as many reps as you can with each condition), a very similar degree of muscle growth may be produced by 6-rep sets, 30-rep sets, or anywhere in between. Correspondingly, the bent knee variation that provides less resistance can still be just as productive for developing the abdominals as the straight knee variation for most people when leaving the same number of reps in reserve for sets of either exercise. The one exception may be for individuals with especially strong abdominals who are able to perform a very high number of bent knee leg raise reps per set before reaching a pronounced degree of muscle fatigue, indicating that the resistance from these reps are very low relative to their strength. At some point an exercise may just be providing too low of a resistance to optimize how much muscle growth that a set can stimulate even if the set is performed until failure. Specifically where that threshold lies is currently unclear, but that should not be any concern for you unless you are able to perform over 30 reps per set, which will not be an issue for the vast majority of people performing bent knee leg raises with a controlled technique through a full range of motion.
